# Bodies, Robots & the Data Flywheel

*לזכרה של עלמה ז"ל*

## The thesis, in one paragraph

A large language model learns everything it knows by reading — it has never picked up a cup, never felt a hinge stick, never had to catch something before it hit the floor. That's a real ceiling: the internet is full of words about the world but almost empty of the moment-to-moment sensor-and-muscle data that tells you *how* to act inside it. Robots fix that by generating their own training data every time they move — a camera frame, a force reading, a motor command, over and over, millions of times a day, for free, forever. The dream driving robotics right now is a **flywheel**: put enough robots to work, however clumsily at first, let their attempts become tomorrow's training data, use that data to make them less clumsy, deploy the improved robots to collect even better data, and repeat. If it spins up, the flywheel does for physical intelligence what scraping the web did for language — except this data source never runs out, because the real world keeps generating fresh situations no dataset could contain in advance.

## The real mechanism

**Why robots need a different training recipe than chatbots.** A language model's "actions" are just more text, so text you scraped is already the right training signal. A robot's actions are torques and trajectories, and there is no pre-existing internet of those. Robotics research over the last decade has converged on three ways to manufacture that missing data: *simulate it*, *have a human demonstrate it cheaply*, or *pool it across many different robots at once so nobody has to collect it alone*.

**Simulation and the sim-to-real gap.** Training entirely inside a physics simulator is fast and safe — you can run thousands of parallel trials on a GPU cluster instead of waiting for one real arm to move. The catch, named the "reality gap," is that policies that ace the simulator often fail on the real machine because friction, sensor noise, and cable slack were never modeled quite right. Tobin et al. (2017, OpenAI/Berkeley) showed a fix called **domain randomization**: instead of trying to simulate reality perfectly, randomize textures, lighting, and physics parameters so wildly during training that the real world just looks like one more variation the policy already handled. OpenAI pushed this to a striking result in **Dactyl** (OpenAI et al., 2019), a robot hand trained almost entirely in simulation that learned to solve a Rubik's Cube one-handed, transferring to the physical hand with no real-world fine-tuning. NVIDIA's **Eureka** (Ma et al., 2023) automated a further piece of this pipeline, using a large language model to write and iteratively improve the reward functions that shape simulated training — a sign that the "recipe design" step of robot learning is itself starting to be automated.

**Cheap human demonstration.** The other route skips simulation and just makes it cheap for a person to show the robot what to do. **ALOHA** (Zhao, Kumar, Levine & Finn, 2023) built a bimanual teleoperation rig from roughly $20,000 of off-the-shelf parts and showed it could collect enough demonstrations for a robot to learn fine motor tasks — threading a zip tie, flipping an egg — that used to require expensive industrial hardware. **Mobile ALOHA** (Fu, Zhao, Finn et al., 2024) put that same rig on a rolling base so the demonstrations could include walking around a kitchen. Going further, Stanford's **UMI, Universal Manipulation Interface** (Chi et al., 2024) removed the robot from data collection entirely: a person holds a handheld gripper rigged with a GoPro and wanders around cooking, cleaning, folding — and that in-the-wild footage becomes training data for a robot that was never in the room. Both lines feed policies like **Diffusion Policy** (Chi et al., 2023), which generates smooth, multimodal action sequences instead of committing to one brittle trajectory at each instant — a much better match for how messy real demonstrations actually look.

**Pooling data across many different robot bodies.** The single biggest structural idea of the last two years is that a policy doesn't have to be trained from one robot's data alone. The **Open X-Embodiment** collaboration (2023–2024, a consortium spanning Google DeepMind, Berkeley, Stanford, CMU and dozens more labs) merged data from 22 different robot embodiments — different arms, grippers, cameras, degrees of freedom — into one dataset and showed that a single model, **RT-X**, trained across all of them transfers skill *between* robot bodies better than any robot's own data could alone. That result matters enormously for the flywheel argument: it means the data collected by a warehouse arm in one lab can make a humanoid in another lab better, so the flywheel isn't 100 separate small wheels, it's one big one everyone feeds. Google's earlier **RT-1** (Brohan et al., 2022) and then **RT-2** (Brohan et al., 2023) pushed this further by initializing the robot policy from a vision-language model pretrained on ordinary internet images and text, so the robot inherits some notion of what a "banana" or "empty bowl" is before it ever picks anything up — internet knowledge and physical practice fused into one model. DeepMind's **RoboCat** (2023) closed an early, small version of the actual flywheel loop: a generalist policy that practiced a new task, used its own best attempts to generate more training data for itself, and improved through several such self-training rounds without a human writing new demonstrations each time.

**Foundation models built for the body.** By 2024, several groups had stopped training one policy per task and started training general-purpose "vision-language-action" models meant to be fine-tuned onto any robot, the way GPT is fine-tuned onto any chat task. **Physical Intelligence's π0** (2024) and **NVIDIA's GR00T** project are both explicit attempts at this: a single large pretrained policy, absorbing internet-scale visual and language knowledge plus large cross-embodiment robot datasets, meant to be the "base model" a company drops onto its own hardware rather than the thing every startup reinvents from zero.

## Where this is going (2025–2030)

The near-term story is economics catching up to the algorithms. Humanoid hardware costs have fallen fast enough that companies like **Figure AI** (which partnered with OpenAI in 2024 to put a vision-language-action model on its Figure 02 humanoid), **1X Technologies** (whose NEO robot leans explicitly on a learned "world model" rather than hand-coded control), and **Tesla's Optimus** program are all racing to put general-purpose humanoids into real workplaces — warehouses and factories first, because those environments are more controlled and the economic case is easiest to make. Expect the next five years to look less like one dramatic breakthrough and more like the fleet-learning playbook that autonomous driving already proved: put imperfect robots to real, supervised work; log everything; retrain centrally; push improved policies back out; repeat on a shortening cycle. Cross-embodiment pooling (the Open X-Embodiment idea) should keep expanding, meaning smaller companies increasingly train on shared or licensed multi-robot datasets rather than building one from scratch — a real structural shift, similar to how few companies today train a language model on their own text alone. Watch also for the fusion of world-model research with robotics: DeepMind's **Genie** (2024) showed that a model can learn a controllable, playable environment purely by watching video, and **SIMA** (2024) showed one policy following language instructions across many different simulated 3D games — both are early signs that "practice environments" for robots may increasingly be *learned* from video rather than hand-built by engineers, closing part of the sim-to-real gap by making the sim itself a better model of reality. This is also where the "data wall" argument connects directly back to robotics: researchers such as Villalobos et al. (2022, updated 2024) have projected that high-quality public text for language-model pretraining could be exhausted within this decade. Embodied data doesn't have that ceiling — every robot hour manufactures new, non-repeating sensorimotor data — which is exactly why so much investment is now flowing toward physical deployment as the next scaling substrate, not just the next product category.

## The honest limits

Say plainly what is not yet true. **No flywheel has actually closed itself at scale.** Every real deployment today still depends heavily on human teleoperators, human labelers, or human supervisors correcting the robot when it fails — the loop is closer to "robots assist humans who generate the training data" than "robots generate their own training data unsupervised," and nobody has published evidence of a robot fleet that keeps improving indefinitely without that human scaffolding thinning out over time. **There is no agreed scaling law for robot data** the way there is for language-model tokens and parameters — nobody can yet say with confidence how many robot-hours of a given task buys how much reliability, which makes the economics of "just collect more" far murkier for bodies than for text. **Contact-rich and deformable tasks remain hard**: cloth, cables, food, anything that deforms or where friction dominates still breaks policies that handle rigid objects fine, and the sim-to-real gap for these cases has not closed the way it has for rigid-body manipulation. **Reliability is far below human level** — a factory worker's hands don't fail one time in five hundred; many current learned policies still do, which is disqualifying for most unsupervised commercial use. **Evaluation is not standardized**: different labs report success rates on their own tasks and hardware, so cross-paper comparisons are unreliable, and claims of "generalist" performance should be read cautiously until independently replicated. And the humanoid form factor's economics — whether a two-legged, two-armed general robot ever beats task-specific machines on cost per unit of useful work — is a genuinely open bet, not a settled conclusion, however confident current company marketing sounds.

- **Open X-Embodiment Collaboration, "Open X-Embodiment: Robotic Learning Datasets and RT-X Models" (2023/2024)** — the paper that proved data from many different robots can be pooled into one model that transfers between bodies; the structural key to the whole flywheel argument.
- **Zhao, Kumar, Levine & Finn, "Learning Fine-Grained Bimanual Manipulation with Low-Cost Hardware" (ALOHA, 2023)** — shows how cheaply real demonstration data can now be collected, which is the honest bottleneck the flywheel has to clear.
- **Ha & Schmidhuber, "World Models" (2018)** — the clean, foundational read on training an agent inside a learned simulation of its own experience; the conceptual ancestor of Genie, SIMA, and every "practice in imagination" idea now feeding back into robot learning.
