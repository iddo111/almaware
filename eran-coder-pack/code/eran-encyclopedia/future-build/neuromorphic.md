# Physical AI: Weights You Can Touch

Every neural network you have ever used — GPT, Stable Diffusion, the recommendation engine that picks your next video — is a set of numbers, "weights," stored in ordinary digital memory and multiplied against other numbers by an ordinary digital processor, billions of times a second, shuttling data back and forth between the memory chip and the compute chip the whole way. That shuttle is the single biggest reason AI eats so much electricity. Now imagine a different kind of chip: one where the weight is not a number sitting in memory waiting to be fetched, but a physical property of a tiny piece of matter — how easily current flows through a sliver of metal oxide, how strongly a switch remembers the last pulse it received. Multiplication happens not by computing but by Ohm's Law itself, current times conductance, in the same instant and the same place the number "lives." That is the promise of physical AI: computers where the substance of the chip does the arithmetic, not a program running on top of it. It is one of the real answers to "what happens after Moore's Law stops handing us free speed."

## The real mechanism

The idea is old, and the pioneer who named it did so deliberately against the grain of digital orthodoxy. **Carver Mead**, in "Neuromorphic Electronic Systems" (*Proceedings of the IEEE*, 1990), argued that biological neurons compute with continuous, noisy, low-power analog physics — not with clocked binary logic — and that silicon could be built the same way: transistors run in their subthreshold regime, behaving less like switches and more like the leaky, graded membranes of real neurons. Mead's lab built analog retinas and cochleas that did real-time sensory processing at a tiny fraction of the power a digital equivalent would need. "Neuromorphic" as a field starts there.

The circuit element that makes physical *memory* possible was proposed even earlier, in theory. **Leon Chua**, in "Memristor — The Missing Circuit Element" (*IEEE Transactions on Circuit Theory*, 1971), showed on purely mathematical grounds that a fourth basic circuit element should exist alongside the resistor, capacitor, and inductor — one whose resistance depends on the history of current that has flowed through it. Nobody had a real device to point to for 37 years. Then **Dmitri Strukov, Gregory Snider, Duncan Stewart, and Stanley Williams** at HP Labs published "The missing memristor found" (*Nature*, 2008): a titanium-dioxide thin-film device whose resistance shifted, and stayed shifted, depending on the polarity and duration of the voltage pulses driving it. Push current one way and the device gets more conductive; push it the other way and it gets less conductive; stop, and it holds that state — indefinitely, with no power. That is a synapse in hardware: a physical scalar that remembers.

The leap from single device to computer came from wiring memristors into a **crossbar array** — a grid of horizontal and vertical wires with a memristor at every intersection. Feed a voltage into each row, and every device along a column pulls current proportional to its own conductance; Kirchhoff's current law sums those contributions automatically at the column's end. That is a matrix-vector multiply — the single most common operation in a neural network — performed not by a processor stepping through multiply-accumulate instructions but by physics settling into equilibrium, all rows in parallel, in the time it takes current to flow. **Mark Prezioso, Farnood Merrikh-Bayat, Brian Hoskins, Gina Adam, Konstantin Likharev, and Dmitri Strukov**, "Training and operation of an integrated neuromorphic network based on metal-oxide memristors" (*Nature*, 2015), built a real 12×12 memristor crossbar and used it to classify simple noisy patterns — the first working demonstration that a physical crossbar could learn, not just store. **Peng Yao and colleagues** at Tsinghua later scaled the idea to a "Fully hardware-implemented memristor convolutional neural network" (*Nature*, 2020), running actual image recognition end-to-end on memristor hardware rather than in software simulation of memristor behavior.

A second, complementary lineage skips crossbars and copies the *timing* code of real neurons instead. **Paul Merolla and colleagues at IBM**, "A million spiking-neuron integrated circuit with a scalable communication network and interface" (*Science*, 2014), built TrueNorth: a chip of a million digital "neurons" that communicate with discrete voltage spikes, only drawing power when a spike actually fires, sitting essentially idle everywhere else — a very different energy budget than a GPU that toggles every transistor on every clock cycle whether or not there is anything meaningful to compute. **Mike Davies and colleagues at Intel**, "Loihi: A Neuromorphic Manycore Processor with On-Chip Learning" (*IEEE Micro*, 2018), pushed further by putting the learning rule itself on-chip — synaptic weights that adapt locally, using only information available at that synapse, the way biological plasticity is believed to work, instead of shipping activations back to a separate training run.

A third lineage asks for even less engineering. **Herbert Jaeger's** echo-state-network report (GMD, 2001) and **Wolfgang Maass, Thomas Natschläger, and Henry Markram's** "Real-Time Computing Without Stable States" (*Neural Computation*, 2002) independently showed that you do not have to design a network's internal dynamics carefully at all. Throw an input into almost any sufficiently rich, nonlinear, recurrent physical system — a random tangle of neurons, a bucket of water, a length of optical fiber — and its natural transient response already contains a rich nonlinear mixture of the input's recent history. You only need to train a simple linear readout on top. This is **reservoir computing**: let the physics of the "reservoir" do the hard nonlinear work for free, and photonic groups such as **Kristof Vandoorne and colleagues**, "Experimental demonstration of reservoir computing on a silicon photonics chip" (*Nature Communications*, 2014), have shown it running at the speed of light in an actual chip.

## Where this is going (2025–2030)

The field is shifting from "can a lab demo classify digits" to "can this replace a GPU rack for a real workload," and the honest answer is: for inference, in narrow but commercially real slices, yes, already. IBM's **NorthPole** chip — Dharmendra Modha and colleagues, "Neural inference at the frontier of energy, space, and time" (*Science*, 2023) — keeps an entire neural network's weights on-chip next to the compute instead of fetching them from external memory, and reports roughly 25× the energy efficiency of a comparable 12-nm GPU on ResNet-50 inference; IBM has since demonstrated it in a 3U VPX form factor aimed at edge and defense deployment (2024), which is the sign of a research chip becoming a product. IBM's analog phase-change-memory line, building on **Geoffrey Burr and colleagues**, "Neuromorphic computing using non-volatile memory" (*Advances in Physics: X*, 2017), reached a 64-core mixed-signal in-memory-compute chip for DNN inference (Le Gallo et al., *Nature Electronics*, 2023) — the direct descendant of the crossbar idea, scaled toward production silicon. Expect this trajectory to continue: not analog chips replacing GPUs for training frontier models, but analog and near-memory chips taking over specific, power-constrained inference jobs — always-on sensor processing, edge vision, hearing-aid-scale audio models, satellite and drone payloads — where every milliwatt is a real design constraint and 8-bit or lower precision is tolerable. Startups such as **Mythic** (analog compute-in-flash) and **Rain AI** (analog neuromorphic inference, publicly backed by OpenAI's Sam Altman in 2023) are betting the same bet at commercial scale. Intel's **Loihi 2** (2021) and the academic **SpiNNaker** machine (Steve Furber, University of Manchester, "The SpiNNaker Project," *Proceedings of the IEEE*, 2014) continue to push spiking hardware toward larger, more brain-scale simulations used for neuroscience research and ultra-low-power robotics control, not general chat models. Photonic reservoir and photonic matrix-multiply accelerators (Lightmatter, Lightelligence, and academic groups following Vandoorne 2014) are the most plausible route to analog hardware actually touching *training*, since light can carry a matrix multiply at near-zero marginal energy once the optics are built — the open question is whether the optical hardware and the electronics around it can be manufactured cheaply enough at scale. Watch for hybrid chips — a normal digital CPU or GPU tile sitting next to a memristor or phase-change crossbar "co-processor" for exactly the matrix-multiply-heavy layers — as the pragmatic middle path through the rest of the decade, rather than a wholesale replacement of digital logic.

## The honest limits

This is real hardware doing real computation, not vaporware — but the gap between "works in a paper" and "runs your workload" is still wide, and it is worth naming precisely where. Device-to-device **variability** is the biggest one: no two memristors, even built side by side on the same wafer, switch at exactly the same voltage or settle to exactly the same conductance, so a crossbar's actual matrix multiply is always a noisy approximation of the ideal one, and every chip needs extra calibration or error-correction circuitry to compensate. **Endurance and drift** are close behind — some memristor materials degrade or drift in value after thousands to millions of switching cycles, which is fine for a weight that is set once and read forever (inference) and a real problem for a weight you want to update continuously (on-chip training). **Precision** is limited: analog signals are cheap to compute with but expensive to read out accurately, so most analog accelerators today operate at the equivalent of 4–8 bits, well below the 16–32 bits standard digital training uses, which caps how directly you can port an existing trained model onto the hardware without retraining or fine-tuning for it. There is **no mature software stack** — nothing like CUDA's decades of compiler and library investment exists yet for memristor crossbars or spiking chips, so today most of these systems are trained in ordinary software and only the finished, frozen weights are "programmed" onto the physical device — true on-chip learning at scale, with backprop-quality credit assignment, remains an open research problem, not a shipped capability. And manufacturing yield at the scale modern models need — billions to trillions of parameters — has not been demonstrated for any exotic-device crossbar; every big number quoted above is for chips with millions to low billions of synapses, not the frontier-model scale digital hardware already reaches. Say plainly: physical AI is proven at the inference-accelerator scale and unproven at the frontier-training scale, and nobody yet knows how far up that ladder analog physics can climb before its own noise floor becomes the limit.
