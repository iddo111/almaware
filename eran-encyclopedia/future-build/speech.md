# Learning to Speak From Raw Sound

Every human baby learns to talk the same absurd way: nobody hands it a chart of phonemes, nobody labels the sound waves, it just flails a wet, floppy set of muscles — lips, tongue, jaw, larynx — listens to the noise that comes out, and slowly discovers which flails make noises that get a reaction. It is learning to *play an instrument it cannot see*, purely from the sound it makes and the sound it hears around it. For seventy years, machine speech went the opposite way: engineers built a phoneme table by hand, and every synthesizer and recognizer was forced through that human-designed alphabet. That is starting to change. A new generation of AI systems learns speech the way the baby does — from raw audio only, no transcript, no phoneme list — and when researchers peek inside these systems, something striking shows up: without ever being told what a tongue or a lip is, the model's internal representation lines up with the actual physical shape of a mouth. The frontier isn't a better phoneme table. It's a machine quietly inventing its own mouth.

## The real mechanism

**Step one: throw out the labels.** Self-supervised speech models learn directly from raw waveforms with no transcript at all. **wav2vec 2.0** (Baevski, Zhou, Mohamed & Auli, 2020) masks spans of raw audio and trains the network to identify the correct hidden chunk among distractors, forcing it to build its own internal vocabulary of sound units. **HuBERT** (Hsu et al., 2021) pushes further: it iteratively clusters its own hidden representations into pseudo-labels and re-trains against them, bootstrapping a discrete inventory of speech units purely from statistical regularities in the waveform — no linguist involved. Run either model on enough raw audio and clusters emerge that line up strikingly well with real phonemes, discovered from scratch.

**Step two: those units turn out to be articulatory.** This is the surprising part. Cheol Jun Cho, Peter Wu, Abdelrahman Mohamed and Gopala K. Anumanchipalli, in "Self-Supervised Models of Speech Infer Universal Articulatory Kinematics" (2023), took the hidden layers of models like HuBERT and wav2vec 2.0 — trained with zero linguistic supervision — and asked a simple question: can a *linear* map recover the actual physical trajectory of the tongue, lips, and jaw (recorded with electromagnetic articulography, small sensor coils glued to a speaker's tongue) from these hidden activations? The answer was yes, and it transferred across speakers and even across languages the model had never explicitly modeled. The implication: a network trained on nothing but "predict the missing sound" converges on something close to a representation of *how the sound was physically made*, not just what it sounded like. Cho and Anumanchipalli's follow-on system, **SPARC** ("Speech Articulatory Coding," 2024), turns this into a full articulatory codec — encoding speech into interpretable articulatory-style features and resynthesizing it back into audio through a neural vocoder, giving a controllable, mouth-shaped intermediate representation instead of an opaque one.

**Step three: the articulatory bottleneck already works as a bridge from the brain.** The clearest proof that "route speech through a simulated mouth" is a genuinely good idea, not just an academic curiosity, comes from neuroscience rather than NLP. Gopala K. Anumanchipalli, Josh Chartier and Edward F. Chang, in "Speech synthesis from neural decoding of spoken sentences" (*Nature*, 2019), decoded cortical activity recorded directly from the speech-motor areas of patients' brains — not into text, and not into raw audio directly, but into an intermediate space of vocal-tract kinematics, which was then synthesized into audible, intelligible speech. Going through the "how would a mouth move to make this sound" step, rather than jumping straight from neurons to waveform, was what made the decoding work well. The UCSF group's later work (Metzger et al., 2023) extended the same lineage toward real-time neuroprostheses, including a talking avatar, for people who have lost the ability to speak.

**Step four: build a spoken language model with no text anywhere.** A separate, parallel line of work asks what happens if you skip transcripts not just for the acoustic model but for the *entire* language model. Kushal Lakhotia and colleagues at Meta AI, in "On Generative Spoken Language Modeling from Raw Audio" (2021, the "GSLM" paper), took HuBERT-style discrete speech units, treated them as if they were a made-up "pseudo-text," and trained a standard language model directly on sequences of them — then re-synthesized the model's continuations back into audio. The system could complete a spoken sentence with a plausible next clause, entirely without text ever entering the pipeline. Zalán Borsos and colleagues at Google, in "AudioLM" (2023), extended this with a two-tier scheme — coarse "semantic" tokens for what is being said, plus finer acoustic tokens for how it sounds, voice and all — producing longer, more coherent continuations. Eugene Kharitonov and colleagues pushed the same textless approach to full two-party conversation in "dGSLM" (2022), training a model on raw two-channel dialogue audio that learned to reproduce turn-taking, backchannels ("mhm"), and even overlapping speech and laughter, none of which were ever labeled.

**Step five: ground it the way an infant does — with eyes, not dictionaries.** A baby doesn't learn the word "ball" from a definition; it learns it from a ball being present while someone says the sound. David Harwath and James Glass's group modeled exactly this: "Unsupervised Learning of Spoken Language with Visual Context" (Harwath, Torralba & Glass, 2016) and the follow-up "Jointly Discovering Visual Objects and Spoken Words from Raw Sensory Input" (Harwath et al., 2018) trained networks on nothing but images paired with raw spoken descriptions, and word-like acoustic units emerged spontaneously, anchored to the objects they named — again with no transcript, no dictionary, no phoneme table.

**Step six: model the babbling stage itself.** Underneath all of this sits an older, smaller-scale but very literal question: can a computational model reproduce the actual first months of an infant discovering its own vocal tract? Anne Warlaumont and Elika Bergelson (as Warlaumont & Finnegan), in "Learning to Produce Syllabic Speech Sounds via Reward-Modulated Neural Plasticity" (2016), built a simple simulated vocal tract driven by a neural controller, gave it a reward signal loosely modeled on the way caregivers respond more to speech-like sounds than noise, and watched canonical babbling — repeated syllable-like sounds — emerge from that self-organizing loop, with no phonetic targets ever specified. It is a small, deliberately minimal echo of the exact process this chapter is about: sound out, listen, adjust.

## Where this is going (2025–2030)

- **Merged articulatory + discrete-unit models.** Expect the two threads above — GSLM/AudioLM-style discrete "pseudo-text" language modeling and SPARC-style articulatory codecs — to fuse into systems that generate speech by simulating how a mouth would move, then rendering the acoustics, rather than selecting phonemes from a fixed inventory. That buys controllability current systems don't have: dial an accent, simulate a speech disorder for clinician training, or resynthesize a voice from partial, degraded input.
- **Silent-speech and clinical neuroprosthetics.** The Anumanchipalli/Chang articulatory-bottleneck approach is the direct ancestor of ongoing brain-to-speech neuroprosthesis work (Metzger et al., 2023, and Stanford's parallel Willett et al., 2023 line). The 2025–2030 trajectory is toward faster, more natural, more portable devices for people who have lost speech to ALS, stroke, or laryngectomy — this is one of the few corners of speech AI where the clinical deployment path is already real, not speculative.
- **Low-resource and endangered-language documentation.** Because self-supervised articulatory and acoustic-unit models don't need transcripts, they are the natural tool for languages with little or no written corpus — a direct continuation of the Zero Resource Speech Challenge lineage (Dunbar, Dupoux and colleagues, 2017 onward), which was explicitly framed as "what would a machine need to do to learn language the way an infant learns it, from audio alone."
- **Voice-preserving assistive tech.** Compact, interpretable articulatory codes make it realistic to "bank" a person's voice from a small amount of their own raw speech — no transcript required — before a degenerative condition takes it, then resynthesize it later. This is a near-term, unglamorous, high-value application of exactly the mechanism above.
- **Audio-visual grounding at scale.** The Harwath/Glass image-speech grounding line is being scaled up toward full video — a model that watches lips and faces move while it hears the resulting sound, inferring articulation visually as well as acoustically, closer to how an infant has both a face to watch and ears to listen with.

## The honest limits

- **Correlation, not proof of mechanism.** The "universal articulatory kinematics" result (Cho et al., 2023) is a *linear probe* finding — a straight-line map recovers tongue/lip/jaw position from the model's hidden state. That is real and reproducible, but it is not proof that the network is internally *simulating* a vocal tract the way a physical model would; it shows the information is linearly present, not how it's used.
- **Ground truth is scarce.** Training and validating articulatory inference still leans on electromagnetic-articulography recordings — real tongue-coil data — which exist for only a small number of speakers and are heavily skewed toward English. "Universal" claims rest on a thin evidence base.
- **Textless language models are still shallow.** GSLM, AudioLM, and dGSLM produce fluent-*sounding* continuations, but they trail text-trained language models badly on anything requiring semantic reasoning, and their coherence over more than a few seconds is weak. They are proof that spoken language modeling without text is possible, not that it is yet competitive.
- **No end-to-end infant.** Nobody has a single system that starts from silence, babbles, listens to itself and to caregivers, and arrives at a full adult phonology of any language purely through self-supervised audio feedback with a physically simulated vocal tract. Warlaumont & Finnegan's babbling model is a genuine proof of concept at the scale of a handful of vowel-like sounds — not a path yet built to the full acquisition timeline a real child completes in a few years.
- **Unsupervised discovery still lags supervised transcription by a lot.** Successive Zero Resource Speech Challenge results have narrowed the gap in unsupervised phoneme and word discovery, but accuracy and unit purity remain well below what supervised automatic speech recognition achieves — and further below the speed and reliability of human infant acquisition.
- **The same misuse risks as any voice AI.** Articulatory and acoustic-token models generate genuinely realistic speech; the consent and voice-cloning concerns that apply to any modern TTS system apply here too, and nothing about the "raw sound" framing changes that.
