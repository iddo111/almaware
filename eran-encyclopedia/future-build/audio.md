# Neural Audio & the Machine Voice

*Neural audio codecs, audio-token language models, and the road to a machine that can speak, sing, and remember a voice.*

## Overview

Sound feels continuous — a smooth pressure wave hitting your eardrum — but every machine that generates it now treats it as something closer to a sentence: a string of discrete tokens, drawn from a small learned vocabulary, predicted one at a time by a language model exactly like the model that predicts the next word in a chatbot reply. That single move — turning raw waveform into "phonemes for machines" — is why the same transformer architecture that learned to write essays also learned to clone a voice from three seconds of audio, compose a two-minute pop song from a text prompt, and hold a real-time spoken conversation with normal human interruptions. The codec is the tokenizer; the audio model is just GPT wearing headphones.

## The real mechanism

### From waveform to token: the neural codec

A CD-quality second of audio is 44,100 numbers. No transformer can predict a sequence that long token-by-token and stay coherent, so the first job is compression — not lossy-MP3 compression for human ears, but compression into a *symbolic* alphabet a language model can predict. That job belongs to the neural audio codec, and its lineage is short and well documented.

It starts with **WaveNet** (van den Oord et al., 2016), DeepMind's autoregressive model that predicted raw audio sample-by-sample with dilated convolutions — proof that a neural net could generate convincing speech and music directly from waveform, at the cost of being agonizingly slow (one sample at a time, thousands of samples per second). The efficiency breakthrough came from a different DeepMind paper the following year: **VQ-VAE**, "Neural Discrete Representation Learning" (van den Oord, Vinyals & Kavukcuoglu, 2017), which showed an encoder-decoder network could learn a *discrete* latent code — a small learned "codebook" of vectors — that compresses continuous signals (images, speech, audio) into a short sequence of integers a model can predict, then reconstructs the original from those integers with a decoder.

Turning that idea into a production-grade audio codec took two more steps, both from industry labs racing each other in 2021–2022. Google's **SoundStream** (Zeghidour, Luebs, Omran, Skoglund & Tagliasacchi, 2021) combined a convolutional encoder with *residual vector quantization* (RVQ — stacking several codebooks, each one coding the error left by the last) and an adversarial (GAN) loss, so the decoder had to fool a discriminator into believing its reconstruction was real audio. That adversarial training is why modern neural codecs sound clean at very low bitrates where older codecs (like MP3's psychoacoustic coding) turn to mush. Meta answered with **EnCodec**, "High Fidelity Neural Audio Compression" (Défossez, Copet, Synnaeve & Adi, 2022), open-sourcing a similar RVQ-GAN codec that compresses 24kHz audio down to roughly 1.5–24 kbps while staying intelligible and natural, and it became the default tokenizer for a wave of open audio-LM research. Descript's **DAC** ("Improved RVQGAN," Kumar et al., NeurIPS 2023) pushed fidelity further with better quantizer training, and Kyutai's **Mimi** codec (used in Moshi, 2024) pushed the other direction — toward very low latency, streaming, single-codebook tokens cheap enough to predict fast enough for real-time conversation.

### From tokens to language model: audio as a second language

Once a waveform is a sequence of discrete tokens, everything the LLM world already knows about sequence modeling transfers directly. Google's **AudioLM** (Borsos et al., 2022) was the paper that made this explicit: it modeled audio generation as a pure next-token-prediction problem over codec tokens, using a hierarchy of coarse "what's being said" tokens and fine "how does it sound" tokens, and produced speech continuations coherent enough that listeners could not reliably tell them from real recordings of the same speaker. The same group's **SPEAR-TTS** (Kharitonov et al., 2023) showed the technique needed almost no paired text-audio data to learn to read text aloud. **MusicLM** (Agostinelli et al., 2023) took the identical recipe — text tokens in, audio tokens out — and pointed it at music instead of speech, generating minutes-long compositions from prompts like "a calming violin melody backed by a distorted guitar riff." Meta's open-source answer, **MusicGen** (Copet et al., 2023), did the same with a single-stage transformer over EnCodec tokens, small enough to run on a single GPU.

The same architecture cracked voice cloning. Microsoft's **VALL-E** (Wang et al., 2023) treated text-to-speech as codec-token language modeling and discovered that, given just a **three-second** audio prompt, the model could continue speaking in that speaker's voice, including their emotional tone and the recording's background acoustics — something older TTS systems needed minutes of clean studio audio to approximate. **YourTTS** (Casanova et al., 2022) had already shown zero-shot voice cloning was possible with a more traditional flow-based architecture; VALL-E showed the codec-LM approach did it with far less speaker-specific engineering. Meta's **Voicebox** (Le et al., 2023) generalized further, using non-autoregressive flow matching over audio tokens to do text-to-speech, denoising, and style transfer from one model. By 2024, ByteDance's **Seed-TTS** and a wave of commercial systems (ElevenLabs, OpenAI's voice products) had pushed cloned-voice naturalness to the point where blind listening tests routinely fail to separate synthetic from recorded speech for short clips — this is a real, repeatedly demonstrated capability, not a marketing claim.

Underneath all of it sits one more real, load-bearing piece of infrastructure that made the whole field possible in the other direction — turning speech *into* text at scale: OpenAI's **Whisper** (Radford et al., 2022), trained on 680,000 hours of weakly-labeled multilingual audio, which is the ASR backbone a huge share of today's voice pipelines and voice-annotation datasets are built on.

## Where this is going (2025–2030)

**Real-time, full-duplex conversation.** The 2023-era voice assistants took turns: you spoke, it thought, it replied. The frontier now is full-duplex — a model that listens and speaks *simultaneously*, can be interrupted mid-sentence, and reacts with sub-second latency, because the streaming codec and the language model run in the same low-latency loop rather than a pipeline of separate ASR-LLM-TTS stages. Kyutai's **Moshi** (Défossez et al., 2024), released as an open weights research system, is the clearest public demonstration: two audio streams (its own and the listener's) predicted jointly by one model, with a genuine ~200ms reaction time and audible non-speech reactions (laughter, "mm-hm"). OpenAI's GPT-4o voice mode and Sesame's **CSM** (Conversational Speech Model, 2025) point the same direction from the commercial side — natively audio-in, audio-out models rather than text models bolted to a separate voice box. Expect this to become the default shape of voice assistants by the end of the decade: not "speech recognizer feeding a chatbot," but one model that thinks in sound.

**Singing and long-form music.** Text-to-music moved from ten-second clips (MusicGen, MusicLM) to full songs with vocals, structure, and mixing — the trajectory visible in commercial systems like Suno and Udio, which by 2024 could produce a radio-length song with a coherent verse-chorus structure from a text prompt in under a minute. That commercial leap also produced a real, citable legal fight: the RIAA, on behalf of major labels, sued both Suno and Udio in June 2024 for training on copyrighted recordings without a license — a fight over provenance and consent that is going to shape how the next generation of these models is trained, not just how they perform.

**Voice as a lasting, personal object.** Few-shot cloning (VALL-E's three-second prompt, YourTTS's zero-shot speaker embedding) is what makes "memorial models" of a specific person technically reachable rather than science fiction: given a real person's recordings — an hour, ideally more, of them actually speaking, in their own words, across different moods — a codec-LM voice model can be fine-tuned or conditioned to continue speaking in a way that preserves their timbre, cadence, and characteristic turns of phrase. Combine that with an audio-driven talking-face model and a text model trained or grounded on what the person actually wrote and said, and the pieces exist today, separately, to build something that sounds and speaks like someone who can no longer speak for themselves. Nobody has shipped a rigorously validated version of that as a single system yet — that is a real, honest "not yet," not a modesty performance — but every component (few-shot voice cloning, streaming low-latency generation, expressive prosody control) that such a system would need is now a published, working method rather than a hope.

## The honest limits

We do not yet have reliable *emotional* and *prosodic* control — models clone a speaker's average voice well, but steering a specific emotional performance ("say this line as if you just heard bad news") on command, rather than by accident of the prompt audio, remains brittle and inconsistent across systems.

Singing voice synthesis still lags behind trained human singers on sustained pitch stability, vibrato, and breath control across a full song; text-to-music systems generate a convincing thirty seconds far more reliably than a structurally coherent, hook-repeating, mixed and mastered four-minute track.

There is no accepted way yet to *detect*, at scale and reliably, whether a piece of audio was generated — watermarking schemes exist and are being deployed by some labs, but they are not universal, not tamper-proof, and not something a listener can check themselves, which is a real open safety gap, not a solved problem.

The legal and ethical status of training data (the Suno/Udio suits above) and of voice cloning of real people without consent is unresolved in most jurisdictions — some countries have started regulating AI voice cloning for fraud and robocalls specifically, but general consent and posthumous-likeness law has not caught up to what the models can already do.

And a cloned voice, however accurate the timbre, is not evidence the model has anything like an internal model of *how that person thought* — it copies acoustic and prosodic patterns, not judgment, memory, or personality; a "memorial voice" that sounds exactly right can still say things the real person never would have, and we have no rigorous way yet to measure how often that happens or how to bound it.
