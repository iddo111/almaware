# From the Perceptron to Now

## The one-paragraph version

In 1958 a psychologist built a machine that could look at a shape and learn, on its own, to tell it apart from another shape — not because anyone programmed the rule, but because the machine adjusted its own internal numbers every time it got the answer wrong. That single idea — a system that turns mistakes into automatic corrections to a big pile of adjustable numbers called weights — is still, sixty-plus years later, the entire engine under every chatbot, image generator, and self-driving system you've heard of. Everything that happened between 1958 and today is one long argument about three questions: how big a pile of weights do you need, how do you correct them efficiently, and what do you feed them to learn from. The argument isn't over. That's the honest, interesting part.

## The real mechanism

**The first machine (1958).** Frank Rosenblatt's Mark I Perceptron read a 20x20 grid of photocells, multiplied each pixel by an adjustable weight, summed the results, and fired "yes" or "no" past a threshold (Rosenblatt, 1958, *Psychological Review*). When it fired wrong, a simple rule nudged the weights toward the right answer. It could learn to separate anything that was *linearly separable* — anything you could draw a single straight line through, on a graph, to split the two classes apart.

**The winter (1969).** Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's book *Perceptrons* proved, with unforgiving rigor, that a single-layer perceptron mathematically cannot learn XOR — the simplest possible pattern that isn't linearly separable (Minsky & Papert, 1969). The book didn't kill the idea of layering perceptrons — Rosenblatt himself had already sketched multi-layer designs — but it killed the funding and the optimism. Research money moved to symbolic, rule-based AI for most of the 1970s.

**The comeback (1986).** The missing piece was a way to *train* multiple layers stacked on top of each other. David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams showed that the calculus chain rule lets you push an error signal backward through every layer and compute exactly how much each weight, however deep, contributed to the mistake — backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, 1986, *Nature*). Multi-layer perceptrons could now solve XOR and much harder problems. This is the moment "neural network" stopped being a dead end and became a method.

**Learning to see and remember (late 1980s-1990s).** Yann LeCun applied backprop to convolutional networks — layers that share the same small set of weights across an entire image instead of learning a separate weight per pixel — and read handwritten zip codes and bank checks with it (LeCun et al., 1989; LeCun, Bottou, Bengio & Haffner, 1998, LeNet-5). Separately, Sepp Hochreiter and Jürgen Schmidhuber solved a different problem — plain recurrent networks forgot everything more than a few steps back, because the gradient signal shrank to nothing as it was pushed backward through time. Their Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) added gates that let a network choose what to keep and what to forget (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997). For fifteen years, LSTMs were how machines handled speech, translation, and any data with order.

**Deep learning ignites (2012).** Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton trained a deep convolutional network — "AlexNet" — on ImageNet using GPUs, and it beat every hand-engineered computer-vision system by a shocking margin (Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, 2012, NeurIPS). The ingredients weren't new — convolution and backprop were both decades old — what was new was enough labeled data (ImageNet's 14 million images) and enough cheap parallel compute (gaming GPUs) to make depth pay off. This is the moment the field's center of gravity shifted from "cleverer algorithm" to "more data and more compute, applied to an architecture that scales."

**Attention and the transformer (2014-2017).** Sequence models kept needing to "look back" further than an LSTM's gates comfortably allowed. Dzmitry Bahdanau, Kyunghyun Cho, and Yoshua Bengio introduced *attention* — letting a model directly weigh every earlier position when producing the next output, instead of squeezing the whole past into one fixed memory vector (Bahdanau, Cho & Bengio, 2015). Ashish Vaswani and seven co-authors at Google then asked a bold question: what if you threw away the recurrence entirely and built a network out of nothing but attention? The Transformer could look at an entire sequence at once, in parallel, which meant it could be trained enormously faster on enormously more text (Vaswani et al., 2017, "Attention Is All You Need"). Every major language and multimodal model since — GPT, BERT, Gemini, Claude, Llama — is a transformer.

**Pretrain, then talk (2018-2020).** Once the transformer existed, two teams found two different ways to exploit it. Jacob Devlin and colleagues at Google pretrained a transformer to fill in masked-out words across huge amounts of unlabeled text, producing BERT — extraordinarily good at *understanding* language (Devlin et al., 2019). OpenAI, starting with Alec Radford and colleagues, instead trained a transformer to simply predict the next word, over and over, across ever-larger text collections — GPT, then GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2018; Radford et al., 2019). GPT-2's authors initially delayed full release, worried about misuse of a model that could produce fluent fake text — a small preview of debates that would recur at every larger scale.

**Scale becomes a science (2020-2022).** Jared Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI showed that a model's loss falls in a smooth, predictable curve as you increase parameters, data, and compute together — a genuine scaling law, not a hunch (Kaplan et al., 2020). OpenAI used that logic to train GPT-3, a 175-billion-parameter model that could perform new tasks from a handful of examples in its prompt with no retraining at all — "few-shot learning" simply falling out of scale (Brown et al., 2020, "Language Models are Few-Shot Learners"). Two years later, DeepMind's Jordan Hoffmann and colleagues discovered that most large models of the era were actually *under-trained* relative to their size — a smaller model trained on proportionally more data (their 70-billion-parameter Chinchilla) beat much larger ones on a fixed compute budget (Hoffmann et al., 2022). Scale, it turned out, had its own internal arithmetic, and getting the ratio of parameters to training tokens right mattered as much as raw size.

**Making it useful and polite (2022).** A raw next-word predictor is not a helpful assistant — left alone, GPT-3 would happily continue a question with more questions. Long Ouyang and colleagues at OpenAI fine-tuned GPT-3 using human preference rankings and reinforcement learning — RLHF, building on the technique Paul Christiano and colleagues had introduced for simpler control tasks (Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022, "InstructGPT"). The resulting model, wrapped in a chat interface, launched as ChatGPT in November 2022 and became the fastest-adopted consumer software product in history. Jason Wei and colleagues also documented that some abilities — arithmetic, instruction-following, chain-of-thought reasoning — didn't scale smoothly, but appeared abruptly once a model crossed a certain size, an "emergence" pattern still debated (Wei et al., 2022a, "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models"; Wei et al., 2022b, "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning").

**Reasoning as a second training loop (2024-2025).** GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2023) and its rivals pushed pretraining scale further, but the next real architectural shift was giving models a *second* training signal, on top of pretraining: reward the model for reasoning chains that reach a correct, verifiable answer — mostly in math and code, where "correct" can be checked automatically — using reinforcement learning at training time so the model learns to think longer before answering. OpenAI's o1 (2024) was the first widely deployed version of this; DeepSeek's R1, released openly in January 2025, showed a similar reasoning capability could be trained with a comparatively small, transparent reinforcement-learning recipe, and published exactly how (DeepSeek-AI, 2025, "DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning"). The practical result: models that spend more compute *at answer time* — thinking longer — rather than only more compute at training time, opening a second scaling axis besides "bigger model."

## Where this is going (2025-2030)

- **Test-time compute becomes a dial, not a fixed cost.** The o1/R1 result — spend more compute per answer to get a better answer — is a new scaling lever alongside parameter count and training data; labs are building models that can be told to "think for a minute" or "think for an hour" depending on the problem.
- **Open-weight models keep closing the gap with closed frontier models faster than most predicted.** Meta's Llama line, Alibaba's Qwen, Mistral, and DeepSeek's releases have repeatedly landed within roughly a year of the closed frontier at a fraction of the announced training cost, reshaping who gets to build on state-of-the-art AI rather than just rent access to it.
- **Multimodality stops being an add-on.** Models that natively read, generate, and reason across text, images, audio, and video in one architecture — rather than bolting a vision model onto a language model — are becoming the default shape of a frontier system.
- **Agentic use is the widest gap between demo and product.** Models that call tools, browse, write and run code, and take multi-step actions with limited supervision are the industry's central 2025-2027 engineering problem, not a research curiosity.
- **The compute buildout is physical, not just algorithmic.** Training and inference at this scale is now bottlenecked as much by power, chip fabrication, and data-center construction as by algorithms — Nvidia's move from H100 to Blackwell-generation systems is a direct response, and several labs now openly discuss dedicated power contracts, including nuclear, for the rest of the decade.
- **Robotics and "world models" are the next frontier for grounding.** Yann LeCun's Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture argues that predicting the next word is a poor training signal for understanding the physical world, and pushes instead toward models that predict abstract future states directly (LeCun, 2022) — now being tested at scale in projects like Meta's V-JEPA line and across robot learning generally.

## The honest limits

Nobody credible claims to know when, or even whether, current methods reach human-level general intelligence, and the range of serious public estimates is not a narrow band — it spans decades. Ray Kurzweil has held to a 2029 prediction for human-level AI since his 2005 book *The Singularity Is Near*, and restated it in 2024's *The Singularity Is Nearer*. Dario Amodei's 2024 essay "Machines of Loving Grace" sketches a scenario where a system he calls "a country of geniuses in a datacenter" could plausibly arrive as early as 2026-2027, while stressing this is a scenario, not a forecast. Rodney Brooks has publicly bet the opposite direction for years, arguing AGI is unlikely before 2050 and grading his own predictions against reality on his blog. Yann LeCun has repeatedly and publicly argued that autoregressive large language models — the entire GPT lineage — are structurally the wrong architecture to reach human-level intelligence, no matter how much they're scaled, because they don't build a real predictive model of the physical world. A 2022/2024 survey of thousands of published AI researchers found the group's own median guess for "high-level machine intelligence" swung by decades depending on exactly how the question was phrased (Grace et al., 2024, "Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI") — which is itself evidence that even the people building this technology don't have a shared answer.

What we genuinely do not know, stated plainly: whether scaling pretraining further keeps delivering proportional gains, or whether the field has already picked most of the easy fruit from that particular tree and the future gains come mostly from the newer reasoning/test-time axis instead; whether the useful supply of high-quality text data available on the internet is close to exhausted, a question Pablo Villalobos and colleagues at Epoch AI took seriously enough to model directly (Villalobos et al., 2022, "Will We Run Out of Data?"); whether today's models are actually reasoning or producing a very convincing statistical imitation of reasoning traces seen in training data, a distinction that matters enormously and that the field cannot yet cleanly settle; and whether we can understand, in mechanistic detail, what these models are actually computing internally — interpretability research such as Anthropic's work extracting millions of individually interpretable features from a production-scale model (Templeton et al., 2024, "Scaling Monosemanticity") is genuine progress, but it covers a small fraction of what a frontier model does, and stays behind the curve of what gets deployed. Every one of those four questions has real researchers on more than one side of it. That disagreement is not a communications failure to be smoothed over — it is the honest current state of the science.

## Read next

- **Rosenblatt, "The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain" (1958)** — read the origin document itself; the confident, almost visionary tone of a working scientist describing something he only half understood is worth more than any summary of it.
- **Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" (2017)** — the single paper that architecturally underlies essentially every model in this chapter's second half; short, readable, and the direct ancestor of GPT, BERT, Claude, and Gemini alike.
- **Grace et al., "Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI" (2024, based on a 2022 survey)** — not a technical paper but the best evidence available that timeline disagreement is structural to the field, not a fringe position; read it before trusting any single confident date.
