# World Models & Predict-the-Gist

Imagine trying to catch a ball by predicting the exact color of every pixel it will pass through on its way to your hand. Nobody does that — you predict *where the ball will be*, a compressed, abstract fact, and your hand moves to meet it. That is the bet this whole research thread is making about machine intelligence: instead of training a model to predict the next pixel, the next audio sample, or even the next word, train it to predict the next **gist** — a compact internal summary of what matters — and let it plan and act by imagining how that gist will change. Today's most capable AI systems (large language models) predict the next *token*. This chapter is about the systems being built to predict the next *state of the world*, in a space small enough to reason in and rich enough to act on.

## The real mechanism

**The neuroscience seed.** The idea that brains are prediction machines is old in cognitive science, but it got a precise computational form in Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard's 1999 paper on predictive coding in the visual cortex (*Nature Neuroscience*). Their model treats the cortex as a stack of layers where each layer tries to predict the activity of the layer below it; only the *error* — the part of the signal the prediction got wrong — gets passed upward. Most of the visual world is redundant and predictable (the wall behind you looks the same frame to frame), so a system that only propagates surprise is enormously more efficient than one that re-describes everything, every instant. This single idea — propagate error, not raw signal — is the ancestor of nearly everything below it, later generalized by Karl Friston's free-energy principle and popularized for a general audience by philosopher Andy Clark's *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016).

**The reinforcement-learning branch: learn a world, dream inside it.** David Ha and Jürgen Schmidhuber's 2018 paper, simply titled "World Models," gave this idea a modern, buildable form for agents. Their recipe has three pieces: a **V**ision model (a variational autoencoder) that compresses each observation into a small latent code; a **M**emory model (an RNN) that predicts how that latent code evolves over time, conditioned on the agent's actions; and a tiny **C**ontroller that picks actions using only the latent code and the memory's prediction. The striking result: they trained the controller almost entirely *inside the memory model's own imagined rollouts* — the agent learned to drive a race-track car by dreaming about driving, then transferred the policy to the real simulator with minimal fine-tuning. That "train inside your own dream" trick became the organizing principle for a line of increasingly capable systems from Danijar Hafner and collaborators: PlaNet (2019) showed a learned latent dynamics model (the Recurrent State-Space Model, RSSM) could plan continuous control tasks purely from pixels; Dreamer (2020) and DreamerV2 (2021) scaled this to Atari at human-level scores; and DreamerV3 (Hafner, Pasukonis, Ba & Lillicrap, 2023) became the first algorithm to collect a diamond in *Minecraft* from scratch, with no human demonstrations, using one fixed set of hyperparameters across more than 150 tasks spanning robotics, board games, and procedurally generated worlds. The mechanism in all of these is the same: encode, predict forward in latent space, imagine many possible futures, and pick the action whose imagined future scores best — planning by simulation inside a compressed model of reality, rather than by trial-and-error in the real (or literal simulated) world.

**The self-supervised-learning branch: JEPA.** In 2022 Yann LeCun published "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence," a position paper proposing the Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) as the missing piece for AI that can reason, plan, and understand the physical world the way animals do. The architecture has two encoders — one for the actual input, one (usually an exponential moving average of the first) for the target — and a predictor that, given the embedding of what you *see* plus some context (a mask, an amount of time elapsed, an action taken), tries to predict the embedding of what you *would see*. Crucially, the loss is computed entirely in embedding space: the model is never asked to reconstruct pixels. This matters because reconstructing pixels forces a model to waste capacity on unpredictable, irrelevant detail — the exact texture of grass, the dust on a lens — none of which helps you understand or plan. LeCun's proposal was realized concretely by Mahmoud Assran and colleagues in I-JEPA (2023), which learned strong image representations by predicting the embeddings of masked image regions from visible ones, without any hand-crafted augmentations (no cropping, flipping, or color-jitter tricks — the usual crutches of contrastive learning). Adrien Bardes and colleagues extended the idea to motion with V-JEPA (2024), predicting masked spatio-temporal regions of video to learn representations that capture how objects move and interact, and in 2025 Meta released V-JEPA 2, trained on over a million hours of internet video plus a smaller amount of robot interaction data, explicitly built to support **action-conditioned** prediction — given a current world-state embedding and a candidate robot action, predict the resulting embedding, then pick actions whose predicted futures reach a goal. That is JEPA and the Dreamer lineage converging on the same move: predict a compact future, plan by search over predicted futures, act.

**One shared engineering headache: collapse.** Any architecture that predicts embeddings rather than reconstructing raw input faces a lurking trivial solution — the encoder can cheat by mapping every input to the *same* constant embedding, making every prediction "correct" and the loss zero, while learning nothing. This is called representational collapse, and defeating it without abandoning the latent-space objective is one of the field's real unsolved-in-general problems. Current systems lean on a toolbox of partial fixes borrowed and adapted from self-supervised image learning — stop-gradient targets, momentum/EMA target encoders (as used in BYOL and carried into JEPA), variance and covariance regularization terms — but there is no single accepted theory guaranteeing collapse won't happen at a new scale or on a new modality; it is still discovered empirically, run by run.

## Where this is going (2025-2030)

**Robotics gets a shared imagination.** V-JEPA 2's explicit action-conditioning is aimed squarely at robot planning: a robot arm can use the world model to imagine "if I push this mug left, where will it end up" before committing to the motion, using a world model trained mostly on passive internet video rather than on expensive labeled robot data. NVIDIA's Cosmos platform, released in 2025 as a family of "world foundation models" for physical AI, is being positioned explicitly as reusable infrastructure for robotics and autonomous-vehicle teams — a pretrained, general physical-world predictor that individual labs fine-tune instead of training a simulator-and-policy stack from zero. Expect the same pattern that happened with language models (one giant pretrained model, many cheap fine-tunes) to repeat for physical prediction over this window, with world models increasingly sitting alongside — and in some pipelines replacing — classical physics simulators like Isaac Sim and MuJoCo as the substrate robots practice inside.

**Driving as a world-model problem.** Wayve's GAIA-1 (2023) showed a generative world model trained on driving video could be conditioned on actions (steering, speed) and produce plausible future driving scenes, useful both for training and for stress-testing a driving policy against rare, dangerous scenarios that are too costly to film for real. Its successor GAIA-2 extended this to structured, controllable multi-camera scene generation. The trajectory through 2030 is toward using these models to manufacture the long tail of rare edge cases — a pedestrian stepping out from behind a truck at night in fog — synthetically, at a scale no camera fleet could ever film, precisely because the model has learned the *physics* of driving scenes, not memorized specific clips.

**Interactive worlds as a research tool.** DeepMind's Genie (2024) and Genie 2 (2024) demonstrated something adjacent but important: generating a playable, action-controllable 2D or 3D environment from a single image or text prompt, frame by frame, with no underlying game engine at all — the "physics" is entirely learned. This is a different flavor of world model (generative, pixel-level) from JEPA's abstract-embedding approach, and the two philosophies — generate the world you can see vs. predict only the gist you need — are likely to cross-pollinate rather than one simply winning; expect hybrid systems that keep a JEPA-style latent core for planning while using a generative head only when a human actually needs to see the imagined future.

**Planning without chain-of-thought text.** Perhaps the most consequential shift for the broader AI field: today's reasoning models "think" by generating long stretches of text tokens. A world-model-based agent instead searches over rollouts in latent space — closer to how AlphaZero and MuZero (Schrittwieser et al., 2020) plan Go and chess moves by simulating forward in a learned model rather than narrating in language. As latent world models mature for open-domain, embodied settings (not just board games), expect serious lab investment into agents that plan silently in gist-space and only translate a plan into language or action at the end — potentially far cheaper per unit of "thought" than generating thousands of text tokens, because a latent rollout can be a few hundred small vectors instead of a few thousand words.

## The honest limits

**Long-horizon drift is unsolved.** Errors in a latent prediction compound the same way autoregressive text errors do, and there is no equivalent yet of the perplexity benchmark that let language models track progress unambiguously; "how good is this world model at predicting ten minutes out" is still measured by ad hoc downstream tasks (does the robot succeed, does the driving policy avoid the crash) rather than a standardized metric of latent-space fidelity.

**No public system has closed the loop at internet scale.** DreamerV3 masters diverse *simulated* domains; V-JEPA 2 learns strong video representations and limited robot action-conditioning; nobody has yet shown one model that learns a fully general physical world model from raw multimodal experience (vision, sound, touch, proprioception) at anything close to the scale and generality that GPT-4-class models achieved for text. Whether the same scaling recipe that worked for token prediction will work for gist prediction — more data, more compute, one architecture — is a genuinely open empirical question, not yet answered either way.

**Collapse and evaluation remain craft, not science.** As described above, avoiding representational collapse is still handled by a bag of tricks discovered empirically per-model, and because the useful signal lives in an abstract embedding space rather than human-readable pixels or text, it is hard to inspect *what* a world model believes, or to catch it being confidently wrong, before it fails downstream.

**Sim-to-real and video-to-real gaps persist.** Models trained heavily on internet video (which is filmed by humans, edited, and biased toward interesting events) still underperform when transferred to a real robot's actual sensorimotor stream, echoing the older sim-to-real gap in robotics — a world model trained on watching is not automatically a world model trained on doing.

## Read next

1. **LeCun, "A Path Towards Autonomous Machine Intelligence" (2022)** — the founding blueprint for JEPA and the clearest statement of why predicting embeddings, not pixels, is the proposed path forward.
2. **Rao & Ballard, "Predictive Coding in the Visual Cortex" (1999, *Nature Neuroscience*)** — the neuroscience result that makes "predict the gist, propagate only the error" a biological hypothesis, not just an engineering trick.
3. **Hafner, Pasukonis, Ba & Lillicrap, "Mastering Diverse Domains through World Models" (DreamerV3, 2023)** — the engineering proof that planning by imagining rollouts inside a learned latent model actually works at scale, across 150+ very different tasks, with one fixed configuration.
