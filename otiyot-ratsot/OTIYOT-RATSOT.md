# אוֹתִיּוֹת רָצוֹת
### Letters That Run
**A two-year alphabet program for four-year-olds, built out of movement, song, honey and the body — no screens, no worksheets, no scores.**

> **The promise, in one sentence:** by the first week of כיתה א׳ your child will pick up a pointed Hebrew verse, put a finger on the right-hand end, and read it out loud — and, more importantly, will do it without a flicker of fear, because for two years letters were something he sang, walked, ate and gave away.

**The promise, said honestly, before anything else:** *pointed* text. A siddur, a chumash with nikkud, his own name, אַבָּא. Not a scroll. Reading from a klaf is unvowelled, unpunctuated and chanted from memory — that is a bar-mitzvah skill, not a six-year-old's. Say the wrong version once to a religious parent or an experienced gananet and you lose the room in your first sentence. Say the right one and nobody can argue with you, because it is exactly what cheder has done for eight hundred years.

---

## 1 · The idea, in five sentences

A Hebrew letter is not a picture, it is a **movement** — a hand travelling right-to-left through a fixed order of strokes — and a four-year-old's shoulder can make that movement a full year before his fingers can. Hebrew then hands you three gifts almost no other alphabet gives: the letter *names* carry their own sounds (בֵּית starts with /b/, מֵם with /m/, unlike "double-you"), the vowel is a mark that sits physically *under* the letter so a syllable is one object you can sing, and pointed Hebrew is nearly one-to-one between sign and sound — which is the real reason cheder children decode at five and English-speaking children do not. So we teach five movements instead of twenty-two shapes, we teach the syllable whole and let the child *discover* the vowel by watching only the dot change, and we let the mouth do what a worksheet cannot: you cannot hold a pitch on בּ without a vowel arriving, so the moment a child sings a letter he has already blended. What actually makes cheder work is not a clever method, it is **dose** — a cheder child touches, says or sings a letter for twenty to forty minutes a day; a gan child, by my count, for under a minute — so every device in this document exists for one purpose only, which is to make a small daily dose something a four-year-old volunteers for. And the reward at the end of the loop is never a sticker or a chart: it is honey, four times in two years, and the face of the person who loves him, listening.

## 2 · The room — what is in it, and in what order it arrives

Nothing arrives before the children are hungry for it. This order is deliberate and the last item is deliberately last.

**Week 1 — costs nothing**
- **הַקַּו** — one strip of tape or chalk across the floor, with a red mark at the **right** end. The writing line. Everything else in two years is a rule about this line.
- **The name cards** — one per child, first letter in a different colour, hung where they hang their bags.
- **A voice.** The song is the curriculum. It runs while shoes are tied, on the walk, in the bath.

**Week 1 — costs about 40 shekels**
- **הַמְּנוֹרָה** — one warm lamp with a big satisfying switch, low enough for a four-year-old to reach, low-voltage, weighted so it cannot be pulled over. It is on for letter-time and for nothing else, ever. That is the whole boundary system.
- **Four trays of coarse salt or fine sand** (not semolina — one celiac child ends it). A dustpan and brush beside them; sweeping is the most coveted job in the room.
- **A bucket of 25 mm flat wooden discs** and wax crayons — the day's spark, into a pouch.
- **A pouch and a peg per child.**

**Week 3 — the K2 Plus earns its place**
- **22 grooved letter tiles**, PETG (not PLA — it survives an Israeli windowsill and an alcohol wipe), palm-sized, every edge rounded, ironed smooth. The groove is a fingertip wide and **starts in a deeper dimple that the finger drops into**, getting shallower toward the end — direction is *felt*, not instructed. A green dot is a symbol and four-year-olds do not obey symbols. Five final forms in a second colour.
- **22 small hinged boxes** with the letter on the lid. One sits closed on the shelf all day before its letter arrives.
- **22 letter cutters and moulds** for בצק. This is honestly the best use of his printer in this whole project.

**Week 6 — the CNC**
- **הַמַּסֶּכֶת** — one flat wooden board per child, 22 letter-stations, five shallow grooves at each. This is the visible-knowledge instrument (§7).
- **A name plaque per child**, carved, hanging on his peg for two years and going home at the end.
- **הַסַּפְסָל** — one low step, kept in the corner, used for exactly one thing (§8).

**Month 4 — the beam**
- **A 3–4 m oak or pine beam on the wall**, and a wood-burning pen. Every child gets one line on it, forever. Decide *now*, before anyone reaches it, that every child gets a line — a beam that records who made it is a monument to exclusion that hangs there for forty years.

**Month 6 at the earliest, and only at home — the lighthouse rig**
Never in the circle. See §9. It is an instrument for a father and a measuring tool twice a year, and it is not part of the program.

## 3 · A day — forty minutes, minute by minute

Six children (his neighbourhood group), one adult, ideally a yard and a room. **Forty minutes on the clock; about fourteen of them are actual letter-contact.** That gap is not waste — the walking, the waiting, the sweeping and the being-together *are* what makes a four-year-old come back tomorrow, and tomorrow is the entire mechanism. Say this out loud to yourself, because the temptation to compress it into fourteen efficient minutes will kill it by November.

**00:00 — 00:02 · הַדְלָקָה**
One child, by rota (never as a prize — the moment it is a prize it is a daily disappointment for five children), switches on the lamp.
> «הַמְּנוֹרָה דּוֹלֶקֶת. הָאוֹתִיּוֹת הִתְעוֹרְרוּ.»

**00:02 — 00:05 · מִסְדַּר הַשֵּׁמוֹת**
Name cards. Each child finds his own, and the group claps his name into beats.
> «מִי הִגִּיעַ? נֹ־עָה! שְׁתַּיִם! … יוֹ־נָ־תָן! שָׁלֹשׁ!»
> «מִי עוֹד מַתְחִיל בְּ־מְְ, כְּמוֹ מַתָּן?»

**00:05 — 00:09 · הֲלִיכַת הָאָלֶף־בֵּית**
Everyone holds a knotted rope loop (not shoulders — shoulders is a domino pile-up) and walks a circle, one step per letter, singing the whole 22 in order. When the song reaches a letter that has not yet arrived in the room, everybody stops dead and says, flatly, together:
> «עוֹד לֹא הִגִּיעָה.»
Then jump to the next one that has. Over the year the holes close one by one, and the children start lobbying for the missing letters. That lobbying is the engine.

**00:09 — 00:13 · אוֹת הַיּוֹם**
The box that has been sitting closed since yesterday is opened by today's rota child. Inside: the tile, and three real objects whose names start with its sound. Then the letter's voice, its walk, and the song — **and the letter is called by its SOUND all week, never its name**. Its "big name" is revealed on Friday, like a full name on an ID card.
> «בּ. בּ. בּ. — בַּקְבּוּק. בָּנָנָה. בֶּטֶן.»
> «בֵּי״ת זֶה בַּיִת. יֵשׁ לוֹ גַּג, קִיר, וְעָקֵב שֶׁיּוֹצֵא הַחוּצָה.»

If a child says the name anyway — and he will, from an older sibling, from a poster, from a grandmother — **never correct him**:
> «כֵּן! זֶה הַשֵּׁם הַגָּדוֹל שֶׁלָּהּ. וְהַקּוֹל שֶׁלָּהּ הוּא בּ.»
The moment a child can be *wrong* about a letter, you have built the shame this whole thing exists to avoid.

**00:13 — 00:20 · הַקַּו · outside**
Chalk one letter two metres long in the yard. Green mark at the start, an arrow at the first turn. Everyone walks it **nose-to-tail in a continuous train**, saying the sound on each step — one activity, nobody waiting, and the noise is an asset outdoors. Better still, let the children draw it themselves first; drawing a two-metre letter with a whole arm is more letter-contact than walking it, and chalk is endless.
> «מַתְחִילִים מִיָּמִין. תָּמִיד מִיָּמִין.»

**⚠ The mirror rule.** If you face the children and draw in the air, six children copy a mirrored letter, and in Hebrew — where ב/כ and ד/ר already fight each other — that is a month of damage. Never draw facing them. Hold up a card with the arrow on it, or sit *in* the line facing the same way they face.

**00:20 — 00:32 · תַּחֲנוֹת · three stations, rotating**
1. **מַגַּשׁ הַמֶּלַח** — trace the letter with one finger in the salt, say the sound, shake the tray. The mistake is gone; no evidence of failure survives, ever. That is the whole point of this station.
2. **הָאָרִיחַ** — the grooved tile, traced *inside a cloth bag* so vision is occluded by an object rather than by an instruction (they peek, and asking creates a compliance fight).
3. **הַבָּצֵק** — roll the letter from dough with the mould, or bend it from a pipe cleaner. He keeps it.

While the stations run, the adult pulls **two children at a time** for three minutes of the only thing that actually decides whether these children read:

**הַחִבּוּר — blending.** Sit *beside* the child, shoulder to shoulder, hand over hand. Slide the vowel-stone under the tile together, say it together, then **go quiet in the middle of the syllable and let him finish it alone.**
> Adult: «קָמָץ… בֵּית…»  Child: «בָּ!»
The adult owns the first two beats. The child owns the third and **shouts** it, arms pushing out. From the very first week the child manufactures the blend himself and never merely watches it happen. This is a nine-hundred-year-old drill and the only thing I have changed is who says the loud part.

**00:32 — 00:36 · הַמִּשְׂחָק**
Whichever fits the week:
- **צַיִד הַקּוֹל** — six letters taped on the floor, the adult *makes a sound*, everyone runs to it. This is the only drill in the program that runs sound→letter, which is the direction reading actually goes, and it is worth thirty seconds every single day.
- **הַתְּאוֹמִים** — the confusable twins, one question, everyone answers with their body **without moving their feet** and facing the same wall (so nobody copies sideways): «חַד אוֹ עָגוֹל?» / «יֵשׁ עָקֵב?» / «יֵשׁ חוֹר?»

**00:36 — 00:39 · סְגִירָה**
Each child gets a wooden disc, the adult marks today's letter on it with a crayon, it goes into his pouch. Then the whole room says today's syllable three times — **loud, quiet, whisper** — then three seconds of silence, everyone still. Descending volume lands six four-year-olds better than any instruction to calm down, and it makes the last sound of the day the letter itself.

**00:39 — 00:40 · כִּבּוּי**
A different child switches the lamp off.
> «לְהִתְרָאוֹת, בֵּי״ת.»

### The free dose — the best minutes in the day cost nothing
Four-year-olds spend forty minutes a day waiting: hand-washing, bathroom, before food, before pickup. **Today's sound is the password.** Say it to go outside. Say it to wash hands. Say it to get lunch. Six individual responses per crossing, four crossings a day, zero added minutes, no materials. This probably beats every activity above on seconds-of-contact per child, and it is the part a gananet will adopt without being asked twice.

## 4 · The eight stages, 4;0 → 6;0

Roughly ninety weeks — which is two school years once the chagim eat their share. Every gate is **three unprompted successes on three different days**, never one good afternoon.

---

**שָׁלָב 0 · הַקַּו וְהַפְּעִימָה — The Line and the Beat**
*weeks 1–4 · no letter is taught*
**At the end:** he claps his own name into the right number of beats, he starts at the right-hand end of anything without being told, and he sings the alef-bet while doing something else.
**Signature:** the walking chant with its holes · clapping names and objects into beats (פִּיל=1, שֶׁ־מֶשׁ=2, בָּ־נָ־נָה=3) · the name cards.
**Song:** the alef-bet walk, and nothing else.
**Ready when:** he claps his own name correctly, unprompted, eight times out of ten.
*Why no letters yet: a child cannot blend sounds he cannot hear as separate things. This is four weeks of pure ear, it costs nothing, and skipping it is the most common way these programs quietly fail.*

---

**שָׁלָב 1 · שֵׁשׁ אוֹתִיּוֹת וְקוֹל אֶחָד — Six letters, one voice**
*weeks 5–18*
**At the end:** he gives the *sound* of six letters, out of order, in under three seconds each.
**Letters:** the letters of his own name first, out of sequence — then **א ב מ ש ל ת**.
**Vowel:** קָמָץ and פַּתָּח only. Both say אָ. That is the first rule he learns and it dissolves all later fear of nikkud: *two dresses, one voice.*
**Signature:** the box · the salt tray · the letter road.
**Song:** one melody, one swap-in slot, all 22 letters:
> «בָּאָה אֵלַי אוֹת — מִי הִיא? מִי הִיא?
> **בֵּי״ת! בֵּי״ת! בּ־בּ־בּ!**
> **בַּיִת, בַּקְבּוּק, בָּנָנָה** —
> בּ־בּ־בּ, שָׁלוֹם, **בֵּי״ת**!»
**Ready when:** six sounds, out of order, three seconds, three different days.
*Why these six, and this is the single best arithmetic in the whole design: **א ב מ ש ל ת** plus kamatz gives you* אַבָּא · אִמָּא · שַׁבָּת · שָׁם · בָּא · מָה · שֶׁל. *The first word this child will ever read out loud is the name of the person standing in the doorway hearing it. That is not decoration. That is the entire motivation engine, and it falls straight out of choosing these six.*

---

**שָׁלָב 2 · הַחִבּוּר — The Join**
*weeks 19–28 · no new letters for six weeks, on purpose*
**At the end:** he reads a syllable nobody taught him.
**Signature:** קָמָץ אָלֶ״ף — אָ, every day, child shouts the third beat · **סֻלַּם הַתְּנוּעוֹת** (see below) · the first word: אַבָּא.
**The mechanism, and it is the most important decision in this document:** do **not** teach ש + ָ = שָׁ. Teach שָׁ whole, as one object whose name is "sha" — Hebrew lets you, because the vowel is physically attached underneath and the syllable is one glyph. *Then* keep the letter and change only the dot, and let him watch: שָׁ → שִׁ → שֻׁ → שֹׁ → שֶׁ. **Analysis before synthesis.** Asking a four-year-old to fuse two abstractions he was never able to isolate is where nearly every child stalls, and a well-meaning grandparent saying "just sound it out" will undo this in one afternoon. Write it on the cupboard door.
**Ready when:** he knows בָּ and מָ, you hand him גָּ, and he says it. **The room stops. See §8.**

---

**שָׁלָב 3 · מְנִיפַת הַתְּנוּעוֹת — The Vowel Fan**
*weeks 29–42*
**At the end:** given any letter he knows, he reads it with any vowel.
**Letters:** ה · י · ו · ר · ס · נ
**Vowels:** חִירִיק (אִי), then חוֹלָם (אוֹ), then שׁוּרוּק (אוּ). One new vowel per fortnight, never faster.
**Signature:** the vowel ladder — five mats on the floor, one consonant card above, hop from mat to mat and shout · **חָמֵשׁ הַדּוֹדוֹת**: five baskets far apart across the yard, sort the nikkud cards into them by *sound*, at a run.
> «כָּל הַשְּׂמָלוֹת הָאֲדֻמּוֹת אוֹמְרוֹת אָ.»
**Ready when — and this is the test that matters most in the whole program:** given any consonant he knows, he reads it correctly with **all five vowels, in a random order**. That is not memory. That is a rule.
*Why /i/ second: /a/ and /i/ are the two most acoustically distant vowels there are, so the first contrast he must* hear *is the easiest one that exists. Eleven nikkud signs collapse into five sounds — that is not a simplification, in Israeli Hebrew it is simply true, and it is the largest single reduction of load available anywhere in this curriculum.*

---

**שָׁלָב 4 · הַמִּלָּה — The Word**
*weeks 43–54*
**At the end:** he reads a two-syllable pointed word he has never seen.
**Letters:** **ם** and **ן** pulled forward out of the final-form family, because Hebrew words end in them constantly and without them he cannot read שָׁלוֹם, יָם, שָׁם. Plus צֵירֵי/סֶגּוֹל (אֶ), which is cheap by now.
**Signature:** **רַכֶּבֶת הַהֲבָרוֹת** — every child has two syllable cards on the floor in front of him, and on the drum beat all of them slide their own two cards together and shout the word. (Two children walking toward each other is lovely and seats two children; this seats everybody.) · **הֲלִיכַת מִלָּה** — step leftward, one step per syllable, on a clapped beat · **מִלָּה שֶׁלֹּא הָיְתָה בָּעוֹלָם** (below).
**Ready when:** eight of ten *novel* pointed words correct.
*The never-seen word is the only honest test in existence of whether he is decoding or remembering a picture — but run it as monster-naming, never as a card in silence: «בָּאָה חַיָּה חֲדָשָׁה. אֵיךְ קוֹרְאִים לָהּ?» — לָסִי. מוּנָה. שֶׁבָּר. Ten seconds, laughter, over. And let him write one for you, and get it hilariously wrong sometimes.*

---

**שָׁלָב 5 · מִשְׁפַּחַת הַגַּג — The Roof Family**
*weeks 55–66*
**At the end:** he tells ד from ר, ה from ח, ב from כ, without hesitating.
**Letters:** ד · ח · כ/ך · ג
**This stage exists because five of the most frequent letters in Hebrew — ד ר ה ח ת — are one shape with one small part changed, and nearly all Hebrew letter confusion lives there.** The alef-bet order manufactures this confusion by putting them next to each other. We separate them by *months*, and each new one arrives as a **change the child makes with his own hands** to a shape he already owns:
- **ר** is the plain roof and leg. Round shoulder, no corner.
- **ד** is ר with a **heel** poking out behind, and a sharp elbow.
- **ה** is ר plus a short **left leg that does not touch the roof** — you can slide a hand through the gap. *(Not "resh with the leg broken off" — that describes ה against ח, and a wrong story told at four survives for years.)*
- **ח** is ה with the gap **closed**. No hand fits.
- **ת** is ח **kicking a foot out to the left.**
**Signature:** felt strips on a taped floor shape, one set per child, changing one letter into the next with their hands · the twins game, feet still, facing the wall · always ending by holding up the two **printed** letters and letting them point. That last line is the only part that touches paper and it is the part that counts.
**Ready when:** twelve mixed cards of the family, ten right, twice.
*If a child later swaps ד and ר, it is a scheduling error, not a child error.*

---

**שָׁלָב 6 · כָּל הַמִּשְׁפָּחָה — The Whole Family**
*weeks 67–78*
**At the end:** all 22 letters, all 5 finals, and the two rules.
**Letters:** ז · ט · פ/ף · צ/ץ · ק · ע · שׂ · and ך ן ף ץ taught as **one idea in one session** — "the divers", the letters that drop a leg below the floor at the end of a word. Not five new letters. Never five new letters.
**The two rules, taught once and never laboured:**
- **דָּגֵשׁ** — only ב, כ, פ actually change. Three letters. «הָאוֹת עִם אֶבֶן בַּבֶּטֶן.»
- **שְׁוָא** — at the start of a word it says "eh" (בְּ = be); in the middle it is silent and just closes the syllable (יִשְׁ־מַע). Those two cover about ninety per cent of what he will meet.
**The merged sounds — ט=ת, ק=כ, ע=א, שׂ=ס — are framed as a gift, never a burden:** «אַתָּה כְּבָר יוֹדֵעַ לְהַגִּיד אֶת זֶה. זֶה רַק הַפַּרְצוּף הַשֵּׁנִי שֶׁלּוֹ.» For *reading*, homophony is free. It only costs at spelling age, which is not this project.
**Ready when:** all 22 sounds cold, in random order.

---

**שָׁלָב 7 · הַפָּסוּק — The Verse**
*weeks 79–90*
**At the end:** he reads a new pointed verse of six to eight words, aloud, at ninety per cent accuracy.
**Signature:** daily repeated reading of the *same* short text (the best-evidenced fluency practice there is) · the prefix letters בְּ כְּ לְ מִ שֶׁ וְ taught as **glue**, not as words · the first pasuk: **יְהִי אוֹר. וַיְהִי אוֹר.** Two words, five letters, and it means "let there be light."
**Ready when:** he reads a verse nobody prepared with him.

### The trick that makes six years old realistic
**Choose three pesukim in week 1 of the whole program and sing them every day for two years.** In the car, in the bath, on the walk. Nobody ever mentions that they will one day be read. Then in week 79 one of them appears in print, and he reads a thing he has known by heart since he was four. Decoding a string already in your ear is a *confirmation* task, not a construction task — and this is not a shortcut, it is precisely how leining has always been taught.

## 5 · The 22 letters

**How to use this table.** The **sound** and the **object** and the **song hook** are for every letter, every week, without exception — the acrophonic pictures are not invented mnemonics, they are the letters' own original meanings, which is why the picture-word starts with its own letter's sound and the mnemonic route and the phonetic route point the same way instead of fighting. (This is the classic failure of cute mnemonics: "ג is a dog's leg" retrieves *dog*, which starts with /d/.)

The **body shapes**, though — take eight of them seriously and let the other fourteen be a light gesture inside the song. The eight that carry weight are the ones that are unmistakable or where the contrast is load-bearing: **ל · ס · ש · ח/ה · ת · ב/כ · ד/ר · ק**. A pose that only reads as the letter when you already know it teaches nothing, and twenty-two poses is more than any adult will actually hold in her head.

| אות | הגוף — the body | קול | החפץ בקופסה | וו השיר — the song hook |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **א** | Diagonal windmill: one fist high to the upper-right, one toe low to the left | *silent — the vowel does the talking* | an **empty** box, and a chair | «אָלֶ״ף שׁוֹתֵק — הַנְּקֻדָּה מְדַבֶּרֶת» |
| **ב** ⭐ | Arm over the head reaching left = the roof; body = the wall; **right foot steps out** = the heel | בּ / ב | בַּקְבּוּק · בָּנָנָה | «בֵּי״ת זֶה בַּיִת — גַּג, קִיר, וְעָקֵב» |
| **ג** | Stand on the right leg, left leg kicked forward and frozen mid-stride | ג | גֶּזֶר · גַּלְגַּל | «גִּימֶ״ל רָץ אַחֲרֵי דָּלֶ״ת לָתֵת לוֹ» *(שבת ק״ד)* |
| **ד** ⭐ | Left arm straight out = lintel, body straight down, **sharp elbow**, roof pokes past the post | ד | דֹּב · דֶּגֶל | «דָּלֶ״ת הִיא דֶּלֶת. פִּינָה חַדָּה» |

**ז and ס have no child-friendly picture in modern Hebrew** (zayin is a weapon, samech is a prop). I have given ז a buzz and ס a ring. Choose deliberately and **do not let anyone improvise a cute word that starts with the wrong letter** — that is the one way this table can be broken.

## 6 · The visible knowledge — how a parent *sees* what a child knows

This is the ALMA idea in wood: knowledge you can look at and correct, never a number in someone's notebook, and never a test.

### הַמַּסֶּכֶת — the child's board
A flat wooden board, one station per letter, five shallow grooves at each station. **Five ways of knowing a letter, five different materials**, always laid in the same order:

| חוט | material | what it means |
|---|---|---|
| **פָּנִים** | white wool | I pick this letter out of five |
| **קוֹל** | red cotton | I see it and say its sound |
| **יָד** | blue leather lace | my finger draws it — right start point, right direction |
| **צֵרוּף** | yellow raffia | I join it to a vowel and say the syllable |
| **מִלָּה** | gold cord | I found it in the world and read it in a real word |

Colour **and** texture, so it is readable by a child who cannot read, by a colour-blind grandfather, and in the dark under a blanket. A board, not a necklace — a 60 cm cord with beads is a strangulation risk and a four-year-old will put it over his head no matter what you say. The strung cord version exists once: at graduation.

An adult reads it as a skyline in five seconds and gets a **diagnosis, not a grade**. Real predictions you can check: missing **red** will cluster on א and ע and on the homophone sets; missing **blue** will cluster on letters whose strokes meet (א ע צ ש). And the most important reading in the whole system: **white and red full everywhere, yellow empty** means this child has memorised twenty-two names and cannot decode. Stop adding letters. Teach nothing but the join.

**Three hard rules:**
1. **The board only ever grows.** Never untie a thread. Forgetting at four is normal, fast-recovering and state-dependent — the child who cannot produce ש on Friday morning produces it on Sunday. The honest, shrinkable record lives on the **adult's** card, in pencil, wobbles and all, inside a cupboard. If an adult ever unties a thread in irritation, the instrument becomes a punishment ledger and there is no recovering it.
2. **The board lives closed in his own pouch**, never hung in a row. Six boards on a shelf is a public ranking, and four-year-olds compare compulsively. The legibility is for the parent. The shelf destroys it.
3. **No charts, no stickers, no wall of who-knows-how-many, no end-of-year performance, no video in the WhatsApp group.** A four-year-old converts any visible measure into a race within two days, and the child at the bottom of that race is exactly the child for whom letters most need to stay friendly.

### חוּט הַזָּהָב — the gold belongs to the parent
The four inner threads are tied in the session. **Gold is tied at home, by a parent, and only for a letter the child found out in the world** — a street sign, a yogurt lid, a bus number, grandmother's name on a door. Two instructions to parents, and only two:
- You tie only what you personally heard.
- Instead of «כָּל הַכָּבוֹד», say: **«תַּעֲשֶׂה לִי אֶת זֶה עוֹד פַּעַם, אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לִשְׁמֹעַ.»**

*Do it again, I want to hear it* is delight. *Good job* is a verdict.

### קְרִיאַת הַלּוּחַ — Friday
Once a month the board goes home and the child **reads it out loud** to a parent or a grandparent: finger station by station, sounding every red, singing every yellow, telling the found-word for every gold. The parent's only job is to sit at eye level, put the phone in another room, and be delighted. Nothing is written down. Nothing comes back.

This inverts assessment completely: the parent is not reading a report about the child, the child is showing off to the parent — the same information, delivered as the thing a four-year-old most wants to do. And a parent who does not read Hebrew — Russian, Amharic, Arabic, French — can do this perfectly, because the job is delight, not verification. **Say that explicitly or those families will opt out from embarrassment.**

### The one thing you must build in, or the design quietly rewards the already-loved
**Every child is assigned a named delight-adult** at the start — a parent, a grandparent, an older sibling, an aunt, a neighbour — and that person is told plainly that this is their job. Then a weekly one-line check that every child got his moment from *somebody*. The gold thread, the Friday reading, the parent in the doorway: every one of them fails hardest for exactly the child with an absent or exhausted parent, and the failure is public. That is a real defect in this design and the assignment is the only structural fix I know.

## 7 · The celebration — four thresholds, and the honey

Ceremonies fire **on readiness, never on a date, never in cohorts**. No quota. If you ever find yourself running one on a Thursday because it is nice, the engine is already dead: a reward that becomes expected stops working, and a young child's spontaneous interest genuinely does drop after expected tangible rewards.

**Honey four times in two years. Not five.** Silan or date paste, never bee honey. Drawn on the child's **own** cracker and eaten — never licked off a shared board. Written allergy consent before day one, and a raisin for any child who cannot, offered so casually that nobody sits out the sweetness.

---

**סַף א׳ · הַשֵּׁם שֶׁלִּי** — first weeks
He builds his own name from tiles and says it letter by letter. Then, once: the first letter of his name drawn in silan, and he eats it.
> «שֶׁיִּהְיֶה לְךָ מָתוֹק בַּפֶּה.»
His name is carved into a plaque that hangs on his peg for two years and goes home with him.
*Children own the letters of their own name before any others, so this threshold is one he has already half-crossed — which is exactly why it works as a first ceremony. It feels earned, not granted, and it fires in the first weeks while everything is still being built.*

---

**סַף ב׳ · הַהֲבָרָה שֶׁלֹּא לִמְּדוּ אוֹתִי** — the room echoes you
He knows בָּ and מָ. You hand him גָּ. He says it. **Stop the room.**
He stands on the low step and says the syllable once, loudly — and **the whole room says it back to him in one voice.** Then everyone goes back to what they were doing. No sweets, no certificate, no photograph.

This is the moment the *rule* transferred instead of being memorised, and it deserves the biggest ceremony in the program and gets the cheapest one. Six voices repeating your own sound back at you is enormous at four, it is free, it is repeatable for every child, and it cannot be ranked.

**Two rules.** Never force the step — offer three equally normal forms and let him choose: stand on it, say it from where you are, or the adult announces it and the room echoes while he just stands there and glows. Nobody comments on which he picked. And do not wait to catch this by luck; once a child has two or three syllables, hand him an untaught one every few days. A planned probe with a spontaneous-looking outcome is what good assessment at this age always is.

---

**סַף ג׳ · הַמִּלָּה הָרִאשׁוֹנָה** — the beam
He reads a whole real word aloud for the first time — and because of the six letters we chose, it is almost always **אַבָּא** or **אִמָּא** or **שַׁבָּת**.

The room hears it *when it happens*. That is the real moment. Then, separately and on a scheduled day with no surprise, his delight-adult comes, he reads it again to them, and that word — the word, his name, the date — is burned into the beam on the wall. Second honey.

**Do not phone a parent at 10:00 and make the child reproduce it in a doorway at 16:00 in front of a silent room.** A four-year-old will freeze, refuse or get it wrong roughly a third of the time, and you will have built a public failure on top of his best moment. And if on the day he will not read it aloud, the adult reads it *with* him and the beam is burned anyway.

**Every child gets a line on that beam.** A child who has no word by the end gets his name and his letter. Decide this now.

---

**סַף ד׳ · הַפָּסוּק** — the room stands
At five and a half or six: one whole pointed verse, from a real made object — heavy paper on two turned wooden rollers, so it feels like a thing and not a page. **The room stands up while he reads.** Third honey. Then the board is taken off the peg for the last time and given to him to keep — twenty-two stations, every thread he tied, two years in one object in his hand.

The family chooses the text and nobody comments: a Torah verse (שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא — he will have been singing them all year), or any Hebrew line the child picks, a line from a song, a blessing with his grandmother's name in it. **The power is in the room standing, not in which text is read.** Say that to the adults and the ceremony stays warm for a secular family and unbroken for a religious one.

And scale the text silently: some read a verse, some a phrase, some one word. Same scroll, same standing room, same honey, and nobody in the room is told which is which.

## 8 · Where the technology goes — and where it doesn't

The tracked controller is the thing that started this, and I am going to be straight with you about it, because the ALMA standard says real, with proof, never a demo.

### What the evidence actually supports
Preschoolers who **form letters by hand** recognise them better than those who only look at them or type them, and adding a **touch** channel to letter learning helps. That evidence is about a finger on a surface, with friction and a trace that stays. **There is no evidence I know of that tracing a letter in empty air teaches anything at all** — the persisting visible trace may be doing all the work. The salt tray is the honest ancestor of the wand, it costs twenty shekels, and it is the control condition that would expose this.

### What the wand is not
It is not a station in the room. Six children and one glowing stick means one child holding it and five who are not, and the five are the entire story. And to make it ethical you would have to remove the screen, the score, the beep and the "try again" — which leaves a four-year-old waving a stick while nothing visibly happens. That is not magic to a four-year-old. Magic is what glows, moves, or answers. Expect forty seconds of engagement.

### What the wand actually is — two honest jobs
**One: a father and one child, at home, on a Friday.** He draws the letter in the air, big, whole-arm, and the machine's only product is a **plotted line on paper** that he carries to his mother. Not a score, not a screen — a treasure. And the paper shows a grown-up something no eye can catch: that the stroke started at the bottom instead of the top-right, that it went left-to-right, that the hand stalled at the corner. **That is the ALMA thesis exactly** — not a black box scoring a child, an instrument that shows an adult where the thread broke.

**Two: the measurement in §10.** Ninety seconds per child, twice a year, on start point and stroke direction — the one thing this whole program could genuinely beat everything already on the shelf at, and the one thing nobody currently measures.

### What the printer and the CNC are genuinely for
The grooved tiles. The letter cutters and dough moulds. The boxes. The board. The plaques. The beam. Every one of these has no power, no calibration, no login, and survives being dropped in the sandbox. **This is where his lab belongs in this room, permanently, rather than for one visit.**

### What Eran could eventually be — honestly
Not yet. Wand strokes do nothing for a byte-level syllable model, and a button that claims "now Eran learns from you" when nothing is reaching Eran is theatre — and a four-year-old being lied to by a machine about being a teacher is worse than no button. The emotional mechanic is right and enormously powerful: at four the child is always the one being taught, and one hour a week where **he** is the teacher is a completely different psychological event. Get it for free first — he teaches his little brother, he teaches a puppet that gets it wrong on purpose (an adult drives the puppet and can calibrate the error live to exactly what this child can repair). Revisit the button on the day Eran can hear a letter and say its sound. Then it is real.

### What is not needed, at all
Screens. Apps. LEDs and beeps (a four-year-old will chase an LED instead of reading a letter). Coin cells — never, anywhere, in a room with small children. A progress dashboard. Anything requiring a login, a charger, a calibration step, or a visitor to be present.

## 9 · The smallest real pilot

**Six children, ages 4;0–4;11, four mornings a week, eight weeks.** Six letters — his neighbourhood group, or one friendly gan's afternoon program (smaller group, parents already opted in, no inspector over that slot, staff who are often younger and willing to try things).

**Do this before you build anything:** spend one morning in a cheder and one morning in a gan with a stopwatch and count **one number** — how many seconds a day does one individual child spend actually touching, tracing, saying or singing a letter? My estimate is under forty seconds in a gan and twenty to forty minutes in a cheder. If that gap is real, then the thesis is **dose, not technology**, and the design problem restates itself as "how do I get a four-year-old to five minutes a day" — which is a question about songs and floor chalk and hijacking rituals that already exist. It costs a day and two phone calls, and it hands you the number every later version has to beat.

### The measurement
**Twelve cards: the six taught letters plus six letters you deliberately did not teach.** The child says the *sound* of each. Administered by an adult who did not teach them, at week 0, week 8, and again at week 14 for retention.

**The number is taught-minus-untaught.** The untaught control set is the entire trick — it separates "learned from us" from "grew up eight weeks."

**It worked if:** the gap is at least **3 out of 6**, and it is still there at week 14.
**It did not work if:** the gap is under 2. Then the design is wrong and the children are fine, and you say so as loudly as you would have said the opposite.

### Two more measurements, both free
**Unprompted requests, tallied on the fridge.** If four-year-olds are not *asking* for it by week three, it has failed no matter what the card score says. This is not a soft measure — it is the measure that predicts whether week fifty happens.

**Start point and direction, on plain paper, ninety seconds per child.** Unprompted: does he start the letter in the right place and move in the right direction? That is the specific thing the movement work is supposed to install, and almost nobody measures it.

### And the third variable, which is the one that actually decides everything
**Did the adult show up four mornings a week for eight weeks?** Almost no family completes eighty weeks of daily anything. If the adult missed more than two sessions a week, the honest conclusion is not that the children failed — it is that **the design is too heavy** and must be cut to the version that survives a bad day: the song, the password at the door, and the name cards. Six minutes. Define that floor now and let it officially count, because an all-or-nothing protocol guarantees a gap in week six that becomes a gap in week seven.

## 10 · Honest risks, and what we refuse to do

**The claim, scoped, in writing, before week 1.** Pointed text at six. Not a scroll. And even the true version buys a secular Israeli child less than it sounds: כיתה א׳ teaches decoding in about three months anyway, and decoding without vocabulary is a party trick. The early advantage also fades — children who start at five and children who start at seven look the same by eleven, and Finland starts at seven and reads fine. **What survives is affect.** That he loves letters, that he associates them with fifteen minutes a day of his father's whole attention, that he walks into first grade already at home. That is a real thing to want. It is just not the thing usually claimed, and you should not claim it.

**Shame is the only irreversible failure.** Everything else here can be re-taught. A child who reads at five and flinches at a book is a net loss you cannot undo. **The floppiness, the sudden need for the toilet, the silliness the moment the cards come out — that IS the refusal.** He cannot say "I have had enough" in words at four. Stop for two weeks. Only songs and hunts.

**What we refuse:** screens in front of children. Scores. Sticker charts. A wall of who knows how many. Videos and photos of a child reading, posted anywhere. An end-of-year performance where children read aloud to seated parents with phones up. Correcting a child's articulation inside a chant — ten to fifteen per cent of four-year-olds have unresolved speech sounds and that chant is loud and public. Forcing anyone onto the step. Any activity where one child acts and five watch.

**The child who cannot keep up.** In any six there will be one, and this program must have a place for him that is not a bare board and a missing ceremony. The place is: **more sound play, more repetitions, the same rituals, the same ceremonies, the same line on the beam** — a different job, not a smaller pile. And a stated floor: a child stuck on the same letter for three weeks gets adult time, not more group time. Also permission for a child to sit at the edge and watch, with **nobody commenting**. He joins within a week because it looks fun.

**Check ears and eyes first.** Before anyone says the word dyslexia. Glue ear — persistent middle-ear fluid — is extremely common at four, intermittent, painless, and it makes rhyme and sound work quietly impossible for months. Uncorrected vision is next. Both are cheap, fixable, and routinely missed for a year.

**In Hebrew, dyslexia hides.** Because pointed Hebrew is transparent, a child with a real reading difficulty will often decode **accurately but painfully slowly** — a rate problem, not an error pattern. A parent watching for mistakes will see none and conclude everything is fine. **Watch the clock, not the errors.**

**Two ways a well-meaning adult will break this**, and they should be written on the inside of the cupboard door:
1. Teaching in alef-bet order because it feels right — putting ג next to ד and ה next to ו and starting with the silent letter.
2. Reverting to "just sound it out, שְׁ… אָ… שָׁא" at the join. That is the synthesis route, it is exactly what this design avoids, and it will be done by a grandparent or by you on a tired day.

**Pick one pronunciation in week 1 and never mix.** Israeli Hebrew merges א/ע, ח/כ, ט/ת, כ/ק, ס/שׂ, קמץ/פתח. If the house says one thing and the grandfather says another, the child gets an ambiguous mapping in the exact place where Hebrew is otherwise perfectly deterministic — and that determinism is the whole reason this is learnable at five.

**No pencil at four.** Grip and the fine motor for real letter formation mature around 5;6. Air, salt, dough, rope, chalk, body — yes. Worksheets cost more in affect than they return in skill. And the hand still needs building or he arrives at first grade with letters in his head and no control to produce them: tongs, tweezers, clothespins, threading, tearing paper, painting a wall with water. Two minutes as a warm-up.

**What I invented and what is old.** Old and load-bearing: קָמָץ אָלֶ״ף אָ, honey on the letter (a documented medieval Ashkenazi initiation, and the correct instinct — the first association with a letter should be sweetness), the acrophonic letter meanings, the gimel-running-after-dalet story from שבת ק״ד, the alef-bet chant, one letter's family taught against another. Mine, and untested: the five materials on the board, the sealed box, the hole in the chant, the vowel ladder, the ramped groove, the room echoing a syllable back, the beam. **Treat every invented piece as a hypothesis with a cheap test attached, not as a curriculum.** None of it has met a real four-year-old yet.

## The one thing to do tomorrow morning

Tape a line on the floor with a red mark at the right end. Write his name on a card with the first letter in a different colour. Chalk a two-metre **ל** in the yard — the only letter in Hebrew that rises above the line and is therefore findable at twenty metres — and walk it, saying «לְְְְ» the whole way. Then a tray of salt, then one finger on a printed ל, then one drop of silan on that finger.

> «הָאוֹתִיּוֹת מְתוּקוֹת.»

Then the only test that counts, and it takes twenty seconds: the **next** morning, before anything new, show him a fresh page with eight letters on it he has never seen arranged that way, and ask «אֵיפֹה לָמֶ״ד?» Write down whether he found it first try, and how fast.

That number tomorrow is worth more than everything above it.
